- **CLAIM:** Behavioural data — "the residue of being alive in a watched world" (what a subject looks at, for how long, asks, and does not do) — is posited as a seventh kind of capital, "foundationally": privacy/behavioural sovereignty is architectural, not an add-on ("a value cannot be grafted on; it is foundation, or it is absence"). Behavioural sovereignty is defined as the capacity to act through agents while maintaining irreducible privacy. It is claimed to share the properties of a capital form: generates returns (coordination through trust), compounds over time (reputation builds on reputation), can be invested (privacy architecture as infrastructure), enables opportunity access, and transfers across contexts (portable chronicles). The register carries this as C55 with confidence stated as "architectural" (a category label, not a percentage) and status occupied.

### E4-C03
- **STATUS:** Design-assumption (framing; Promise Theory grounding is Empirical-external per E4-C10)
- **SOURCE:** whitepaper-7th-capital §"The 7th Capital: Behavioral Sovereignty" / §"Extraction Versus Creation" (whitepaper §§565-573, 1543-1571)
- **CLAIM:** Two competing treatments of behavioural data are posed as a dichotomy. The extraction model treats behavioural data as a minable resource (observe, aggregate, sell, value flows away from the subject, privacy consumed in the process). The sovereignty model treats it as renewable capital (curated disclosure through the two-agent split, chronicled agency, value flowing back to the subject through trust-enabled coordination, privacy preserved through the act of creation). The distinguishing predicate is offered as a Promise Theory one: extraction violates the autonomy axiom (a system promising on behalf of a subject without authorisation); creation respects it (each party promises only its own behaviour). Reputation capital is asserted to appreciate while surveillance data depreciates (the latter aligning with the V(pi,t) freshness-decay term e^(-lambda t)).
- **CITATIONS:** none (framing claim; Promise Theory citation carried at E4-C10).
- **FEEDS:** WP-14, WP-15

### E4-C10
- **STATUS:** Design-assumption (market-structure framing) + Empirical-external (IEEE 7012-2025; Promise Theory)
- **SOURCE:** whitepaper-7th-capital §"Invitation vs. Attack" and §"The MyTerms Swordsman" (whitepaper §§229-238, 1322-1424); v4-economics §229 and §361; vrc-protocol-spec §31, §250, §1289 (autonomy axiom)
- **CLAIM:** The market structure is framed as a Promise Theory dichotomy. The INVITATION pattern establishes an acceptance relationship BEFORE any specific proposal; the IMPOSITION (attack) pattern makes a proposal without a prior acceptance relationship. Surveillance is classified as the attack pattern (data extraction begins without prior consent; "accept all" banners are impositions), which "violates the autonomy axiom at scale, promising on behalf of users without authorization" (whitepaper §542). The inverse is instantiated by IEEE Std 7012-2025 ("Machine Readable Personal Privacy Terms", published 2026-01-20), which inverts notice-and-consent: the individual proposes terms and the organisation accepts, negotiates, or declines ("propose and respond", one round maximum, bilateral signed record). The autonomy axiom (agents can only promise their own behaviour) is attributed to Promise Theory (Bergstra & Burgess). Neutral restatement: consent-first bilateral agreement is proposed as the market-structural alternative to unilateral extraction; the standard supplies the machine-readable protocol.
- **CITATIONS:** IEEE Std 7012-2025, *IEEE Standard for Machine Readable Personal Privacy Terms* (2026-01-20); Bergstra & Burgess, *Promise Theory: Principles and Applications* (2nd ed., 2019).
- **FEEDS:** WP-14, WP-15
