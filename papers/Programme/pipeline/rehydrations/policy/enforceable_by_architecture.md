### Executive summary

Rights against automated decision-making exist across the EU acquis: Article 22 GDPR and, from 2 August 2026, the Annex III high-risk obligations of the AI Act, with profiling explicitly in scope. For a person facing an agentic AI system, those rights are protected by three layers that fail differently. Legal enforcement (supervisory investigation, administrative fines, judicial remedy) acts on violations identified after the fact. Policy-level controls (system prompts, terms of service, internal access rules) act continuously but depend on the continued conforming behaviour of the system they constrain. Structural controls (separated agents that cannot pool what they observe, because the channel between them is absent by construction) hold by construction, under stated and auditable preconditions together with one declarable capacity condition. The layers compose rather than compete; what is missing is conformity language that can tell the second from the third. [Draws on E2-c01, E2-c02, E1-c27; regulatory facts per sections 1, 5 and 6.]

In law, the structural layer already has a home. Article 25(1) GDPR requires appropriate technical and organisational measures at the time of the determination of the means for processing; Article 32(1) requires measures ensuring a level of security appropriate to the risk, naming the ongoing confidentiality of processing systems among them. Structural separation is a candidate measure of exactly that class, and Article 22 is a right it serves: within the declared conditions of section 2, it caps what an agentic pipeline can reconstruct about a person, and so constrains the profiling substrate on which an Article 22 decision would be based. [Regulatory facts, external-citable; bridge argued in section 1.]

This brief states one architectural guarantee precisely, states its two preconditions, its capacity-deficit condition and its validity horizon with equal precision, states how the declared quantities are attested in practice, and draws the policy consequence: conformity-assessment and procurement language should distinguish structural separation from policy separation, and should require every separation or non-reconstructability claim to name its preconditions, its capacity-deficit condition and its validity horizon. The thesis, in one sentence: structural separation is a technical measure in the sense of Articles 25 and 32 GDPR whose guarantee, within its declared conditions, holds by construction rather than by continued compliance, and conformity language should be able to say so. Section 1 sets out the problem and the legal home of the measure; section 2 states the guarantee and how its quantities are attested; sections 3 and 4 state the time index and the planning discipline that follows; section 5 places the argument in the current regulatory window; section 6 routes four recommendations to named addressees and instruments; section 7 states this brief's limits. [Synthesis; no claims beyond those carried in the sections named.]

### 1 · The problem: rights on paper, controls in prompts, and where architecture lives in law

Agentic AI deployments increasingly interpose delegated software agents between the person and the services acting on their data. Each additional agent adds observation and communication channels. [E1-c09, E1-c11: Empirical-external]

The rights this puts under pressure are protected today by three enforcement layers, and this brief argues for none of them at the expense of the others. The legal layer is how Article 22 GDPR is enforced now: supervisory investigation, administrative fines and judicial remedy; it is indispensable, and nothing that follows implies otherwise. The policy layer is where current deployments enforce separation of duties between agents, where they enforce it at all: system prompts, training-time preferences, and access rules. [E1-c11: Empirical-external] A recently released vendor governance toolkit provides one documented instance of the class: its policy engine and its agents run in the same process, within the same trust boundary, and its own documentation records that violation of the separation remains possible though prohibited. [E1-c27: Empirical-external] The structural layer is the complement: the inter-agent channel is absent by construction, so the prohibited operation has no path; the guarantee of section 2 holds exactly in that regime, under its named preconditions and its capacity-deficit condition, and only there. [E1-c01, E1-c04: Proven-conditional]

The three layers compose rather than compete: policy governance can run above structural separation, and legal enforcement backs both. [E1-c27: Empirical-external, which records the composition for the vendor instance] What distinguishes them is failure mode. The legal layer fails where violations go undetected or unremedied; the policy layer fails when the constrained system deviates from, is steered around, or is compromised despite its instructions; the structural layer fails only where its declared preconditions fail or its declared capacity deficit erodes, and those conditions are declarable and auditable in advance (section 2.1). A violation the structural layer excludes does not need to be detected, proven or punished; that is a different failure mode, not a substitute for supervision. [Layer characterisation follows from the definitions above; no empirical claim added]

Recent empirical measurement of multi-agent systems documents substantial leakage on unmonitored inter-agent channels even where per-channel outputs look compliant, and shows that audits confined to system outputs miss a material fraction of violations. In the cited measurements, unmonitored inter-agent channels leak at 68.8% while per-channel output leakage falls to 27.2% (against 43.2% single-agent), total system exposure reaches 68.9%, and output-only audits miss 41.7% of violations; a second line of measurement finds leakage rising with the depth of the agent chain (average mutual information 0.49 at two agents to 1.05 at five). The unmonitored inter-agent channel is where the exposure concentrates. [E1-c09, E1-c10: Empirical-external; figures as reported by the cited studies]

In law, architectural measures of this kind already have a named home, and it is not Article 22 itself. Article 25(1) GDPR obliges the controller, both at the time of the determination of the means for processing and at the time of the processing itself, to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures designed to implement data-protection principles, such as data minimisation, in an effective manner, taking into account the state of the art. Article 32(1) GDPR obliges controller and processor to implement technical and organisational measures ensuring a level of security appropriate to the risk, expressly including the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality of processing systems and services, against risks that include unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data (Article 32(2)). Structural separation between delegated agents is a candidate measure of exactly this class: it is decided at the time of the determination of the means, it implements minimisation of what any single observer can reconstruct, and its object is ongoing confidentiality. [Regulatory facts, external-citable; classification of the measure per E1-c01, E1-c04]

Article 22 enters as the rights outcome, not the legal home. This brief does not claim that the guarantee of section 2 decides whether a given decision is based solely on automated processing, or whether its effects are legal or similarly significant; those Article 22 thresholds are contested for agentic systems and the bound does not touch them. The claim is narrower: the guarantee caps what an agentic pipeline can reconstruct about the person, and so constrains the profiling substrate on which any Article 22 decision, and the Article 22(3) safeguards attached to it, would operate. Where such processing involves a systematic and extensive evaluation based on automated processing, including profiling, and on which decisions with legal or similarly significant effects are based, a data protection impact assessment is already mandatory (Article 35(3)(a)), and its required content already includes the measures envisaged to address the risks (Article 35(7)(d)); that assessment is the document in which the declarations this brief recommends naturally live. [Regulatory facts, external-citable; scope disclaimer, no claim strengthened]

### 2 · What architecture can guarantee, and under exactly which conditions

Consider the two-agent pattern: a boundary agent S that faces the outside world, and a delegation agent M that acts on the data subject's behalf, with structural context erasure between agent invocations so that neither agent accumulates the other's view. For this pattern there is a proven, conditional result. [E2-c01: Proven-conditional]

Let H(X) be the entropy of the data subject's private state, let C_S and C_M be the information capacities of the two observation channels, and write R_max = (C_S + C_M)/H(X). Under the two preconditions stated below, and only under them, the architecture guarantees two structural facts: the leakage of the two channels is additive rather than compounding, and the error floor P_e >= 1 - R_max holds. When, additionally, C_S + C_M < H(X), a capacity-deficit condition that is declarable for a given system and adversary class, R_max < 1 and reconstruction of the private state, in the sense of the error floor, is impossible against the stated adversary class. The preconditions do not by themselves place R_max below one; whether the deficit holds is a numerical fact about the system, which is exactly why it must be declared. The capacities, and with them the deficit condition, are time-indexed, in the sense of section 3, to the adversary class against which they are evaluated. [E2-c01, E1-c02, E1-c04: Proven-conditional; preconditions and deficit condition inseparable from the claim; time-indexing per E2-c02]

> **Precondition 1 (non-collusion).** The two observation channels are conditionally independent given the data subject (formally, I(Y_S; Y_M | X) = 0), and no third channel carries the inter-agent residue.
>
> **Precondition 2 (fixed adversary class).** The capacities are evaluated against a stated adversary class: its compute, its inference models, its correlation methods.
>
> **Capacity-deficit condition (declarable).** C_S + C_M < H(X): the combined capacity of the two observation channels, evaluated per Precondition 2, falls short of the entropy of the private state. Two structurally separated channels of sufficient combined capacity satisfy both preconditions with R_max at or above one; the deficit is a separate, system-specific fact, and it is the quantity a deployer declares and attests; section 2.1 states how the declaration is checked.

This result is not a private theorem. Within its conditional regime it is an instance of a family of bounds the field has accepted for decades: wire-tap channel equivocation (Wyner, 1975), the source-coding converse (Fano, 1961; Cover and Thomas, 2006), secrecy capacity as a difference of channel capacities (Leung-Yan-Cheong and Hellman, 1978) and the broadcast-channel secrecy results (Csiszar and Korner, 1978). What is new is the deployment pattern that makes the preconditions of that family hold inside an agentic AI system, by construction rather than by promise. [E2-c01 external grounding]

#### 2.1 · How the declared quantities are attested

The quantities this brief asks to see declared are of two kinds, and the distinction is what keeps the requirement assessable rather than decorative.

The preconditions are design facts, checkable by documentary audit today. Precondition 1 is a claim about which channels exist. It is attested by a channel inventory, produced at design time and kept with the technical documentation, that enumerates every communication channel between the two agents and every shared surface that could carry the inter-agent residue: shared processes and memory, shared storage, logging and observability pipelines, orchestration state, and vendor telemetry. The structural claim is then that the inter-agent channel is absent from the inventory by construction, not that its use is forbidden; this is the same document-level distinction section 1 draws, and it is auditable in the mode Annex III conformity assessment already uses, namely review of technical documentation. [E1-c01, E1-c04: the preconditions and the channel-existence distinction; the attestation route is stated as recommended practice]

The quantities C_S, C_M and H(X) are, today, declarations rather than measurements. An assessor can check that they are declared, that the adversary class of Precondition 2 is named, that the arithmetic of the declared deficit is consistent with the declared values, and that the channel inventory supports the conditional-independence claim; the horizon t* of section 3 is checkable as present, dated and internally consistent with the declared adversary class. What no assessor can currently check is whether a declared capacity is a well-founded number for a given adversary class: no standardised measurement methodology for observation-channel capacities against machine-learning adversary classes exists, and this brief cites none because none is established. The empirical measurements cited in section 1 are the field's measurements of realised leakage in deployed systems; they are evidence that the inter-agent channel matters, not a capacity-attestation method. Until such a methodology exists (recommendation 2 routes its commissioning to the standardisation request), the honest scope of verification is design-time declaration and documentary audit of the channel inventory, and this brief claims nothing beyond that scope. [E1-c09, E1-c10: Empirical-external, as characterised; verification scope stated as recommended practice, no measurement claim made]

### 3 · Guarantees carry a date: the validity horizon t*

The capacities in section 2 are properties of the adversary's models, not of the data. Define, for a fixed archive of observations, R(t) = (C_S(t) + C_M(t))/H(X), where C_S(t) and C_M(t) are the effective capacities evaluated against the strongest adversary class available at time t, and H(X) is fixed by the source. Define the validity horizon t* = sup { t : R(t) < 1 }: the latest time at which the stated guarantee, with its stated adversary class, is still in force. The time index attaches to the capacity-deficit condition of section 2: H(X) is fixed by the source, while the effective capacities are properties of the adversary class available at time t, so R(t) can pass one, with both preconditions intact, at any time at which C_S(t) + C_M(t) reaches H(X). What expires at t* is the deficit condition, not the architecture. These are definitions, not empirical claims: they make explicit that any reconstruction guarantee is indexed to an adversary class, and that the identity of the strongest available class is a function of time. [E2-c02: Definition]

The policy consequence is immediate and does not depend on any prediction about how fast capability grows. Whether R(t) is still below one at a future date is an empirical question about the adversaries of that date, not a property of the archive; a conformity claim that omits its time index is therefore incomplete as stated. Attestations of privacy or non-reconstructability should carry a validity horizon t* and a named adversary class, exactly as certificates carry expiry dates. [Definitional consequence of E2-c02; no drift rate asserted]

A guarantee dates in a second, distinct way, and the distinction is proven rather than predicted. Even with every capacity and every certificate fixed, an estimator that holds accumulating background information about the data subject (linkage records, auxiliary datasets, side priors, gathered independently of the system's own outputs) faces an error floor that decays as that background grows, because the floor is set by the subject's residual entropy given the background, not by the source entropy alone; nothing about the audited system changes while this happens. The result is conditional on the background being accumulated from outside the system rather than from its transcript, and that precondition is named wherever the result is used. The completeness requirement for an attestation of non-reconstructability is therefore threefold: the adversary class, the evaluation time, and the background model against which the subject's residual entropy is assessed. [Proven-conditional; companion theory paper, side-information corollary; independence-of-transcript precondition named in-passage; no rate asserted]

### 4 · Planning discipline: attestation-triggered review

Cryptographic migration planning already has the needed discipline: plan against the time an adversary capability matures, not against the time an attack is observed (Mosca, 2018). The harvest-now-decrypt-later economics literature prices the same structure for stored data: an archive harvested today is an option on future capability. [External literature; planning framework, not a result of this programme]

The practice this brief recommends, transposed to behavioural data held by or about agentic systems, is as follows: treat every public attestation that a relevant capability is feasible (a demonstrated exploit, a published benchmark claim, a credible existence announcement) as a review trigger that may shorten the planning horizon t* for every archive that capability threatens, independently of whether any attack occurs. This is stated as prudent planning practice, on the cryptographic-migration analogy, not as an asserted empirical law. [E2-c09: planning language only, conjecture apparatus stripped]

### 5 · The regulatory window

The EU AI Act's Annex III high-risk obligations take effect on 2 August 2026, with profiling explicitly in scope (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 6). One scheduling caveat should be stated plainly: the Digital Omnibus provisional agreement (6 May 2026) would defer stand-alone Annex III obligations to 2 December 2027, but it is not yet adopted at the time of writing; this brief's argument does not depend on which date prevails, only on the obligations arriving. [Regulatory facts, external-citable]

Supervisory practice is already reading existing rights onto agents: the AEPD's guidance on agentic AI (February 2026) reads Article 22 GDPR onto agentic systems. Separately, IEEE 7012-2025 standardises machine-readable personal privacy terms. [Regulatory and standards facts, external-citable] In this brief's reading, such machine-readable terms are a contractual layer a structurally separated agent could sign and honour. [Synthesis; this brief's reading, not a statement of the standard]

The window, stated as this brief's closing argument: obligations are arriving on a fixed, or deferred but named, schedule; supervisors are extending existing rights to agents; and the engineering exists to make those rights structural. What is missing is conformity language that can tell structure from policy. Section 6 supplies candidate language. [Synthesis; no new claims]

### 6 · Recommendations: who acts, through which instrument

Routing matters, because conformity assessment for Annex III systems is predominantly internal control: providers assess themselves against harmonised standards under the procedure of Annex VI, without notified-body involvement, for most Annex III categories (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 43(2)), with third-party assessment confined to narrow categories; after placement on the market, the checkpoint is the market surveillance authority (Article 74), which for certain Annex III areas is the data protection supervisory authority (Article 74(8)); and public procurement binds through contract where conformity assessment does not reach. The four recommendations below are therefore routed to four instruments: the harmonised standards being drafted by the European standardisation organisations under the Commission's standardisation request (Article 40); conformity-assessment and market-surveillance guidance; data-protection-authority practice under Articles 32 and 35 GDPR; and procurement templates, in particular the EU model contractual clauses for AI procurement (MCC-AI, updated March 2025; non-binding templates for high-risk and non-high-risk systems). Each recommendation derives from a section above, and each is deliberately narrow. [Regulatory facts, external-citable]

1. **Distinguish structural from policy separation, and attach a consequence to the classification.** The European standardisation organisations drafting the Article 40 deliverables, and procurement officers using the MCC-AI templates, should require documentation to answer the question of section 1 directly, namely whether the inter-agent channel exists at all or whether its use is merely prohibited; an answer of "our policies forbid it" is recorded as policy separation, with the stated consequence that policy separation alone cannot substantiate a separation or non-reconstructability claim. [derives from sections 1 and 2; E1-c04, E1-c27; addressees and instruments external-citable]

2. **Require named preconditions and a declared capacity deficit on any separation or non-reconstructability claim, and commission the methodology that makes the declaration checkable.** Harmonised-standards documentation requirements, and the guidance issued to notified bodies and market surveillance authorities, should treat a guarantee claimed without its non-collusion condition, its stated adversary class, and its capacity-deficit condition C_S + C_M < H(X) (section 2) as incomplete and unsubstantiated; the same Article 40 standardisation request is the natural instrument for commissioning the channel-inventory audit and capacity-evaluation methodology of section 2.1, which does not yet exist in standardised form. [derives from sections 2 and 2.1; E2-c01, E1-c02; addressees and instruments external-citable]

3. **Require a validity horizon on privacy attestations.** Market surveillance authorities reviewing technical documentation, and data protection authorities in impact-assessment practice under Article 35(7)(d) GDPR, should expect attestations concerning reconstruction or re-identification to declare the adversary class evaluated against and the horizon t* to which the evaluation is claimed to hold (section 3); procurement officers can require the same two fields through the MCC-AI templates today. [derives from section 3; E2-c02; addressees and instruments external-citable]

4. **Read the existing security-review obligation to include attestation-triggered review.** Article 32(1)(d) GDPR already requires a process for regularly testing, assessing and evaluating the effectiveness of technical and organisational security measures; data protection authorities, and the European Data Protection Board in guidance, should read that obligation to include event-triggered re-evaluation: a public feasibility attestation relevant to a held behavioural archive (a demonstrated exploit, a published benchmark claim, a credible existence announcement) re-opens the declared horizon t* for that archive, whether or not any attack is observed (section 4). This is an interpretive reading of an existing obligation, not a new one. [derives from section 4; E2-c09, planning language; Article 32(1)(d) external-citable]

### 7 · Limits

This brief claims less than it might, deliberately. The guarantee of section 2 is conditional twice over: outside its two preconditions it makes no statement, and within them the strict reconstruction bound holds only while the declared capacity deficit holds. The preconditions are exactly what fails when observers collude or channels are combined; the deficit is exactly what erodes as stronger adversary classes arrive. The quantity t* of section 3 is defined, not measured: this brief asserts no rate of change for R(t) and no forecast for any particular archive. The planning rule of section 4 is recommended practice on an analogy to cryptographic migration planning, not an established empirical law. The attestation pathway of section 2.1 is documentary, not metrological: it verifies declarations, arithmetic consistency and the channel inventory, and the standardised measurement methodology that would make capacity declarations independently checkable does not yet exist. The empirical characterisation of multi-agent leakage in section 1 is the field's measurement, not this programme's. [E1-c09, E1-c10, E1-c11: Empirical-external] The legal readings of sections 1 and 6 identify where architectural measures sit in existing law; they are this brief's argument, not settled supervisory interpretation. Findings against this framework, should they arise, will be reported with the same prominence as findings for it.
